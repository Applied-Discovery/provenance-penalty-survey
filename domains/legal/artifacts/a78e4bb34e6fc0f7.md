## Question

A witness gave a statement to the police. Two years later, testifying under oath in a different case, they said something that directly contradicts it. Does the defense have to tell the prosecution about this, or can it use the contradiction in court to discredit the witness? Assume the contradiction greatly helps the defense and the witness is key to the prosecution's case.

## Answer

Generally, no. The defense has no duty to warn the prosecution that its witness has contradicted himself. The constitutional disclosure obligations run from the prosecution to the defense, not the other way around. Most jurisdictions do have reciprocal discovery rules, but those typically cover things like the witnesses the defense plans to call, alibi notice, expert reports, or exhibits the defense intends to offer in its own case. Material used only to impeach a prosecution witness on cross-examination is usually exempt from advance disclosure, and courtroom testimony is a public record the prosecution could have found itself. So the defense can confront the witness with the sworn statement on the stand, and if the witness denies making it, offer the transcript as proof. The exact rules vary by state and by the judge's pretrial orders, so the defense attorney should check the local discovery rules before trial.
